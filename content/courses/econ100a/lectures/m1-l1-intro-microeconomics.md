## 1. Synthesis

A course-opening orientation video, not a content lecture. It answers two
questions written on the title page and then answers them in order.

**What is microeconomics?** Kristian defines it as *the study of individual
behavior*, where "individual" means either one consumer or one firm. From
there he builds the aggregation chain twice: consumers aggregate into the
**demand side**, firms aggregate into the **supply side**, and the two meet in
a **market**. Markets are then sorted by how competitive they are, presented as
a spectrum with **perfect competition** at one end and **monopoly** at the
other, with **oligopoly** ("fewer firms serving the market") in between. Two
further topics are named as belonging to microeconomics: **game theory**, the
study of strategic situations — defined as those where "what my opponent or my
competitor does affects me" — and **market failures**, attributed to
asymmetric information or externalities.

**What is the structure of this course?** The same four items, now as a
syllabus: consumer theory (preferences plus constraints giving the optimal
bundle, then aggregation into demand), producer theory (cost, technology, the
firm's optimal bundle, aggregation into supply), markets (competitive vs.
market power), and game theory.

The pedagogical move worth preserving is that **the taxonomy and the syllabus
are the same list**. He describes the field, then re-walks the identical
structure as the plan for the quarter. Nothing is defined formally, no notation
is introduced, and there is no example — this video's whole job is to give the
student a map to hang later material on.

## 3. Regenerated script

Rewritten in the tutorIA house style (audio-first, second person, `cue`
markers for synchronized visuals). It keeps Kristian's definitions and his
"same list twice" structure, and gives the taxonomy the visual it never had.

```markdown
# What Microeconomics Studies

<!--cue:individual-->
Microeconomics is the study of individual behavior. Take that word
"individual" literally, but not narrowly: it means one decision maker at a
time. Sometimes that is one consumer deciding what to buy. Sometimes it is one
firm deciding what to produce. The same toolkit handles both, and that is the
first thing worth knowing about this course.

<!--cue:aggregate-->
But a market is not one person. So we do the same move twice. Put all the
consumers together and you get the demand side. Put all the firms together and
you get the supply side. Those two sides meet somewhere, and the place where
they meet is what we call a market.

<!--cue:predict:market_types-->
<!--cue:spectrum-->
Not all markets behave alike, and what separates them is how much competition
there is. Picture a spectrum. At one end, perfect competition: many firms, many
consumers, information moving freely, and no single player able to move the
price. At the other end, monopoly: one firm serving the entire market. In
between sits oligopoly, where only a few firms serve the market — few enough
that each one has to think about the others.

<!--cue:strategic-->
And that is exactly where game theory enters. A situation is strategic when
what your competitor does affects you. When that is true, choosing your own
best action means first working out what the other side is likely to do. Notice
this is not a separate subject bolted on; it is what the middle of the spectrum
forces on you.

<!--cue:failures-->
One more piece. Sometimes markets do not deliver what we would want them to,
and microeconomics has a name for it: market failures. Two common sources.
Information may be spread unevenly, so one side knows something the other does
not. Or there may be externalities, where your decision lands costs or benefits
on someone who was never part of it.

<!--cue:checkpoint:cp1-->
Before we go on: you have just heard four things — consumers and firms, the two
sides of a market, the competition spectrum, and strategic behavior. Can you
say which of them a monopoly is an example of?

<!--cue:syllabus-->
Here is the useful part. That description of the field is also the plan for
this course, in the same order. First, consumer theory: how preferences and
constraints come together to give the best bundle a consumer can reach, and how
consumers aggregate into demand. Second, producer theory: the firm's
technology, its costs, its own optimal choice, and how firms aggregate into
supply. Third, markets — competitive ones first, then the ones where somebody
holds market power. And fourth, game theory, for when the players have to
reason about each other.

<!--cue:recap-->
So: microeconomics studies individual decision makers, consumers and firms.
Those aggregate into demand and supply, which meet in a market. Markets run
from perfectly competitive to monopolistic, with oligopoly in between, and the
less competitive the market, the more strategic reasoning it demands. Market
failures are where the whole thing breaks down. That list is the field, and it
is also the syllabus — we will take it in that order.
```
